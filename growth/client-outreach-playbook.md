## 3. How to qualify a lead (is this a good lead?)

Check these in 2 minutes. Each "yes" = 1 point. **3+ points = good lead.**

| Check | How to check |
|---|---|
| No website, or just a Linktree / Facebook page | Look at Google Maps / Instagram bio |
| Website is slow | pagespeed.web.dev → mobile score below 50 |
| Not good on mobile | Open on your phone. Text tiny? Buttons hard to tap? |
| No booking / no online ordering / no enquiry form | Look for "Book", "Buy", "Get quote" buttons |
| No WhatsApp or chat button | Look at the corners of the site |
| Old design (copyright 2018, broken images, Flash, etc.) | Scroll to the footer |
| They are active (posting on Instagram, good Google reviews, running ads) | Active = they have money and care about growth |

**Bad leads (skip):** business looks closed, no activity for 1 year, huge company with an in-house team, they just launched a new site last month.

## 6. The "free 2-minute video audit" approach

This is your best tool. A short personal video shows you did real work for them. Very few agencies do it.

**How to do it:**
1. Open their website on screen. Start **Loom** (free) with your face in the corner.
2. Say their name and business in the first 5 seconds.
3. Show **3 specific problems**: e.g. speed score, mobile layout, no WhatsApp button, unclear first screen, slow checkout.
4. For each problem, say **why it costs them customers** in one sentence.
5. Say what you would do. **Do not** do the full fix for free.
6. End: "If this is useful, reply and I'll send a simple plan with a fixed price. If not, no problem."

**Script (2 minutes):**
> "Hi {name}, I'm Mohamed from GOFS Tech in India. I was looking at {business}'s website and found 3 quick things that might be costing you customers.
> First, {problem 1} — on mobile, the page took {X} seconds to load / the score is {Y}. Many visitors leave before it loads.
> Second, {problem 2}.
> Third, {problem 3}.
> I've built sites for {similar project, e.g. a commercial kitchen fabrication company / a D2C hair-care brand}, so I know these fixes well.
> If you want, I can send a short plan with a fixed price. If not, I hope this helps anyway. Thanks!"

**Tip:** Loom gives a link with a thumbnail. In email, put a screenshot of the video thumbnail (with their website visible) and link it. People click when they see their own site.

## 7. Rules for cold email and DMs (legal and non-spammy)

**I am not a lawyer. These are simple guidelines. Check the official rules for any country you email often.**

### Always
| Rule | Why |
|---|---|
| **Say who you are** — your real name, GOFS Tech, website | Required by most laws, and builds trust |
| **Write to business contacts only**, about their business | B2B outreach is treated more gently than messages to private people |
| **Make it personal** — mention their business and a real problem | Personal messages are not "spam" in feeling, and get replies |
| **Easy opt-out in every email** — "Reply 'no thanks' and I won't email again." And respect it forever. | Required by US, UK, EU, Australia, Canada rules |
| **Add your business address** in the email footer | **Required by US CAN-SPAM** |
| **Honest subject line** — no "Re:" if it's not a reply, no fake urgency | CAN-SPAM and common sense |
| **Send from a domain email** (e.g. mohamed@gofstech.in), not Gmail | Looks professional, better delivery, protects your main address |
| **Max 2 follow-ups**, then stop | More = annoying |
| **Keep a "do not contact" list** | So you never message them again by mistake |

### Never
- **Never buy email lists.** Often illegal, and they ruin your email reputation.
- Never scrape private personal emails or phone numbers. Use the business contact shown publicly on their site.
- Never send the same copy-paste message to hundreds of people.
- Never send attachments in the first email (looks like a virus). Use links.
- Never add people to a newsletter without their clear "yes".
- Never lie (fake reviews, fake numbers, "we worked with Google", etc.).

### Country notes (simple)
| Place | What to know |
|---|---|
| **USA (CAN-SPAM)** | B2B cold email is allowed if: you identify yourself, honest subject, include a physical address, clear opt-out, and stop within 10 business days of an opt-out. |
| **UK (UK GDPR + PECR)** | Emailing **companies** (e.g. info@ or a named person at a limited company) is generally OK under "legitimate interest" if it is relevant to their job and has an opt-out. **Sole traders and partnerships are treated like individuals** — you usually need consent, so be careful. |
| **EU (GDPR)** | You can rely on "legitimate interest" for relevant B2B messages, but you must explain who you are, why you contacted them, where you found their email, and how to opt out. Some countries (e.g. Germany) are stricter — avoid cold email there for now. |
| **Canada (CASL)** | Stricter. Only email addresses that are **published publicly** by the business, and only about their business role. Always include identity + opt-out. |
| **Australia (Spam Act)** | Similar to Canada: OK only with "inferred consent" — the business has published the address publicly and your message is relevant to their work. Identify yourself + opt-out. |
| **UAE** | Keep it B2B, relevant, polite, and stop if asked. LinkedIn and WhatsApp to published business numbers are common, but don't message repeatedly. |
| **India (DPDP Act 2023)** | Use only business contact details, don't store more data than needed, delete on request. Don't spam WhatsApp — WhatsApp can ban your number. |

### Email setup (do once)
1. Create **mohamed@gofstech.in** (Google Workspace or Zoho Mail).
2. For bigger outreach, use a **second domain** (e.g. gofstech.co or getgofs.com) so your main domain stays safe. (Optional, later.)
3. Set up **SPF, DKIM and DMARC** records in your domain DNS (your email provider shows how — 15 minutes).
4. **Warm up the new inbox:** week 1 send 5–10 emails/day, week 2 about 15–20, week 3 about 25–30. Max **30–50 per day per inbox**, and only when replies and delivery look healthy.
5. Write plain text emails. No images, few links (1–2 max).

## 9. How to price a custom quote

Use this when the project doesn't fit a package (web apps, CRMs, dashboards, bots).

### Step 1 — List every task and estimate hours
| Task | Hours |
|---|---|
| Discovery call + written scope | 3 |
| Design (key screens) | 12 |
| Frontend | 30 |
| Backend + database | 30 |
| Integrations (payments, WhatsApp, email) | 10 |
| Testing on devices | 8 |
| Launch + training | 4 |
| Project management / calls | 8 |
| **Total** | **105** |

### Step 2 — Multiply by your hourly rate
Pick your own rate and write it down so you stay consistent.
Example only: 105 hours × ₹1,200/hour = ₹1,26,000.
(For international clients, use a higher rate — your package prices already show about 2.5–3× the India price.)

### Step 3 — Add a 20% buffer
Things always take longer than planned (client delays, changes, bugs).
₹1,26,000 × 1.2 = **₹1,51,200** → round to **₹1,50,000**.

### Step 4 — Check against your package floor
Custom web apps start at ₹1,20,000 / $4,000. If your number is below this, re-check the scope — you may have missed tasks.

### Step 5 — Give 3 options (the three-option proposal)
People like to choose. Most will pick the middle one.

| | **Essential** | **Recommended** | **Complete** |
|---|---|---|---|
| What | Core features only | Core + the features that save them most time | Everything + automation + 3 months care |
| Example | Lead CRM: add/edit leads, simple dashboard | + WhatsApp alerts, reports, user roles | + AI email drafts, invoice automation, 3 months care plan |
| Price | ₹1,20,000 | ₹1,50,000 | ₹1,95,000 |
| Time | 4 weeks | 6 weeks | 8 weeks |

**Rules:** write the scope clearly. Anything not written = extra (quoted separately). Include 2 rounds of design changes. Never start without the 40% advance.
